How these numbers are made

This app uses two kinds of models. For the things a public survey dataset actually measured, we use a statistical model trained on that dataset. For the things the dataset does not contain, we built our own research-based model from published sun-exposure rules. We keep the two separate on purpose, and we show which is which.

  1. Your profile is converted to the same units and input encoding used during training.
  2. The Gradient Boosting regression model estimates serum 25(OH)D in nmol/L from the core profile features. The sun-habits model additionally uses outdoor exposure and skin reaction when those inputs are available.
  3. The original model prediction is kept separate from the configurable health-adjustment layer. Experimental health deductions and milk bonuses are applied once, then the result is clipped to the configured valid target range.
  4. The dashboard uses the resulting estimate to show a range label, gap-to-target calculation, approximate IU planning guidance, and sun-exposure guidance. These are planning estimates, not measurements of vitamin D in the blood.

It is an educational project. It is not a medical device, not a blood test and not a diagnosis.

How accurate is our model?

Vitamin D prediction is a screening estimate, not a blood-test result. The model predicts serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) in nmol/L from patterns in the US NHANES 2013-2014 survey. It is not trained on Indian participants and cannot replace a laboratory test or clinical advice.

ModelInputs and evaluation results
Basic Gradient BoostingAge, sex, BMI and supplementation. 5,596 adults aged 18-80. Mean absolute error (MAE): 18.04 nmol/L; RMSE: 23.81 nmol/L; R²: 0.255.
Sun-habits Gradient BoostingBasic inputs plus outdoor exposure and skin reaction. 3,541 evaluated NHANES rows aged 20-59. MAE: 17.39 nmol/L; RMSE: 22.74 nmol/L; R²: 0.192.

MAE is the typical size of an error in this evaluation; it is not a guaranteed error range. Individual predictions can be substantially higher or lower than the displayed estimate. R² describes how much variation is explained in this dataset, not clinical accuracy.

How verification was performed

  1. Checked that both saved artifacts are Gradient Boosting regression models with the expected feature lists and valid regression metrics.
  2. Compared predictions with measured NHANES LBXVIDMS values. The sun model achieved MAE 17.0 nmol/L versus 19.5 nmol/L for an always-average baseline on the verification rows.
  3. Checked calibration: groups with higher predicted values had progressively higher observed average vitamin D levels.
  4. Checked feature-direction examples, extreme inputs, realistic output ranges, and the Flask prediction path. The current model verification script passed 22 of 22 checks.

These checks show that the software and saved artifacts behave as expected. They do not prove that the model is clinically validated, causal, or accurate for every person.

Deficiency evaluation: two different cutoffs

The medical evaluation target remains LBXVIDMS < 30 nmol/L. That measured value is the definition of deficiency in every table below. The alternative 54.5/51.1 nmol/L cutoffs are prediction screening thresholds only; they do not redefine deficiency and are not clinical diagnostic thresholds.

What it does not know

Diet, genetics, season and weather, clothing in detail, how much shade you really stand in, and the real response of your own body. Sun time shown is a best case. The survey only tells us patterns across many people. Please check your real level with a blood test and follow your doctor's advice.
