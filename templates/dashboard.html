Vitamin D guide

Short and simple answers: what it does, how to get it, what low levels mean, and what to eat.

What is vitamin D and why do we need it?

Vitamin D helps your gut absorb calcium and phosphorus, so bones and teeth stay strong. It also supports muscle function and the immune system. Your body makes it when UVB rays from the sun hit bare skin. Some foods and supplements also give it.

The blood test that shows your vitamin D status is called 25-hydroxy vitamin D, or 25(OH)D (written in nmol/L or ng/mL; 1 ng/mL = 2.5 nmol/L).

How do we get vitamin D?
☀️ SunFor most people the main source. UVB turns a compound in your skin into vitamin D3. Needs UV index about 3 or more. Window glass blocks UVB, so sitting in sun behind glass does not count.
🍽️ FoodFew foods have much: fatty fish, egg yolk, liver, UV-exposed mushrooms and fortified foods (some milk, oils and cereals).
💊 SupplementsVitamin D3 (or D2) tablets, drops or sachets. Best taken after a blood test and a doctor's advice.

Things that reduce what your skin makes: sunscreen, covering clothes, darker skin, older age, staying indoors, and low UV (early morning, evening, cloudy or polluted air). Studies report that low vitamin D is common in India even though it is sunny, mainly because of indoor lifestyles, clothing and diet.

What do the blood levels mean?

Medical groups do not fully agree on the cut-offs, so ask your doctor how to read your own result.

What happens if vitamin D is low?
  • Children: rickets, which means soft, weak bones, bowed legs and slow growth.
  • Adults: osteomalacia: bone pain, tenderness and muscle weakness. Low vitamin D also makes osteoporosis worse.
  • Older adults: higher risk of falls and broken bones.
  • Over a long time, less calcium is absorbed, so the body takes calcium from the bones.
  • Tiredness and body aches are often mentioned, but they have many other causes. Many people with low levels feel nothing, so only a blood test confirms it.

Higher risk: darker skin, little sun or covered clothing, always using sunscreen, older age, higher body weight, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and conditions such as celiac, Crohn's, kidney or liver disease, or some medicines.

How can I fix it? (ways to fulfil your need)
  1. Sensible sun: short sessions around midday when the UV index is 3 or more, with arms and legs uncovered if that suits you. Stop well before your skin turns pink. This app's burn limit helps with that.
  2. Food: see the food table below. Food alone rarely reaches the daily need unless you eat fatty fish often.
  3. Fortified foods: some milk, edible oils and cereals have vitamin D added. Check the label.
  4. Supplements: the standard daily intake is 600 IU (15 mcg), and 800 IU after age 70. For a proven deficiency a doctor may prescribe more for a limited time. Taking it with a meal that contains some fat may help absorption.
  5. Re-check: if you are treated for low levels, a doctor usually repeats the blood test after a few months.

Can I take too much?

Yes, from supplements (not from sun). The usual upper limit for adults is about 4,000 IU (100 mcg) a day unless a doctor prescribes more. Too much can raise blood calcium and cause nausea, vomiting, weakness, frequent urination and kidney problems. Do not take large doses on your own.

Sun cannot cause this, because the skin stops making extra vitamin D, but too much sun still causes sunburn and raises skin cancer risk.

Limitations of this app
  • It gives estimates only. It is not a medical device, not a blood test and not a diagnosis.
  • The level prediction has a typical error of about 17 to 19 nmol/L, and uses only a few inputs. It does not know your food, genes, medicines, health conditions or season.
  • The model learned from US adults (NHANES 2013-2014), not from Indian people, so it may be less accurate for you.
  • Time outdoors and skin reaction are what you tell the app (self-reported).
  • The UV sensor is hobby grade. It reads at most about UV 7 before it saturates, and the sun maths uses approximate constants.
  • Your daily goal is never below the standard intake (600 IU, 800 IU above age 70), because the model error is large.
  • The prediction is shown only for ages 18 to 80, and the sun model only for ages 20 to 59.
When should I see a doctor?
  • Bone pain, muscle weakness or frequent fractures or falls.
  • You are pregnant, breastfeeding, elderly, or have kidney, liver or gut disease, or take long-term medicines.
  • The "Higher risk" flag appears for you, or you want to start vitamin D tablets (ask for a 25(OH)D blood test first).
  • For babies and children, always ask the doctor about the dose.

General facts follow public health guidance such as the US NIH Office of Dietary Supplements and the Endocrine Society. This is education, not medical advice.

In plain words:
  • The model is fair at ranking risk (ROC AUC about 0.72 to 0.76), but it cannot tell your exact level: the typical error is about 17 to 19 nmol/L.
  • It is not a deficiency detector: it never predicts levels below 30 nmol/L.
  • Its "LOW" label is right most of the time it appears (precision 56 to 64%), but it misses most low people (recall 10 to 26%). The "Higher risk" flag is more sensitive (about 80%) at the price of false alarms.
  • It learned from US data. Use it as a hint, and get a blood test to know your real level.
What do these words mean?
MAE (typical error)
The average gap between the predicted and the real blood level, in nmol/L. Smaller is better.
R²
How much of the differences between people the model explains. 0 = nothing, 1 = everything. Ours is about 0.19 to 0.25, which is low.
Accuracy
Share of all people the model got right. Can look good when one group is much bigger than the other.
Precision
Of the people the model called "low", how many really were low.
Recall (sensitivity)
Of the people who really were low, how many the model found.
Specificity
Of the people who were not low, how many the model correctly left alone.
F1
One number that balances precision and recall.
ROC AUC
How well the model ranks people from lower to higher risk, whatever cut-off is used. 0.5 = guessing, 1.0 = perfect.
Cross-validation
The data is split into 5 parts; each part is predicted by a model trained on the other 4, so the results are honest.
